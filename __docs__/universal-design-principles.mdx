# Universal design principles

These principles apply to everything we design. Each one names what users must be able to do and what it rules out.

**Designs ensure users can…**

1. [Understand every word](#1-understand-every-word)
2. [Act on what they see](#2-act-on-what-they-see)
3. [Always know where they are](#3-always-know-where-they-are)
4. [Stay in control of every recommendation](#4-stay-in-control-of-every-recommendation)
5. [Get to what matters, fast](#5-get-to-what-matters-fast)
6. [Learn it once and use it everywhere](#6-learn-it-once-and-use-it-everywhere)
7. [Mistakes will happen, but avoid errors](#7-mistakes-will-happen-but-avoid-errors)

The [guardrail](#guardrail-for-anyone-anywhere) applies to all seven.

Each principle ends with a **Big 4 check**, which tests a design against the four questions every screen must answer: "What is this?" "Where am I?" "How do I move forward?" "How do I go back?"

## 1. Understand every word

Users can understand every label and message because we use the words they already use.

**Example:** Learner: Mastery shows as "Getting there" or "Mastered," not as a percentage.

**Rules out:**

- Internal names like UMG or AFPM in the UI
- Jargon that sounds precise but reads like a test
- Copy that promises more than the product does

**Big 4 check:** "What is this?" Does each element make sense by itself, in the user's words, without company jargon?

## 2. Act on what they see

Users can act on every insight, because each one ends in a clear next step.

**Example:** Admin: A weekly email names the schools that need attention and links to what to do.

**Rules out:**

- Numbers with no story around them, so the user can't tell what to do next
- Problems listed with no order of urgency, so the user can't tell where to start
- Links that make the user hunt for the action after clicking
- Insights only inside the product when the user lives in email

**Big 4 check:** "How do I move forward?" After reading the data, does the user know what to do about it?

## 3. Always know where they are

Users can always see where they are, what's done, and what's next.

**Examples:**

- Admin: In a multi-step setup flow, the admin always sees which step they're on and how many are left.
- Teacher: The student queue shows where each student is and what's next, and the teacher can click into any student from it.

**Rules out:**

- Removing structure to look cleaner
- Progress that's visible but can't be clicked into
- A long list where a grid would show the whole picture

**Big 4 check:** "Where am I?" Is the "where" obvious, and does the navigation show the current state?

## 4. Stay in control of every recommendation

Users can accept, change, or decline anything we recommend, including anything from AI, and can see why we recommended it.

**Example:** Teacher: Khanmigo suggests regrouping students for tomorrow's lesson, shows the skill results behind it, and the teacher can accept, edit, or dismiss it.

**Rules out:**

- Applying a recommendation the user didn't agree to
- "Recommended" with no reason behind it
- Making the recommended option the only easy option
- AI output presented as fact, with no way to check it

**Big 4 check:** "How do I go back?" Can the user undo or decline a recommendation, and do they know where they are afterward?

## 5. Get to what matters, fast

Users can reach what they came to do without extra steps.

**Example:** Teacher: A teacher who just wants to start using Khan Academy can skip assigning recommended courses during onboarding. Later, it's one click from their dashboard, with no digging through settings.

**Rules out:**

- Setup steps that can't be skipped, or are hard to find again once skipped
- Packing a screen so full that the next action isn't obvious
- Splitting a simple task across screens just to keep each one light
- Cutting steps that prevent costly mistakes (see [Principle 7](#7-mistakes-will-happen-but-avoid-errors))

**Big 4 check:** "How do I move forward?" Is the next best action the most visible thing, and is it clear where it leads?

## 6. Learn it once and use it everywhere

Users can move between any part of the product without relearning, because the words, navigation, and patterns stay the same.

**Example:** Learner: Practice and assessment look like one system, with a locked test mode on top.

**Rules out:**

- A new component when a system one does the job
- The same thing named differently in two parts of the product
- One-off styling for a single page

**Big 4 check:** Would someone who learned the Big 4 answers in one part of the product get the same answers in another?

## 7. Mistakes will happen, but avoid errors

Users can make mistakes, which are part of learning, but never errors, which are caused by our design. When a user makes a mistake, the platform says what happened and tells them how to correct it.

**Example:** Learner: Choosing a wrong answer is part of learning, but a learner never submits one by accident.

**Rules out:**

- Significant actions, like submitting an assessment answer or unlocking a test for a class, that one stray click can trigger
- Messages that say what went wrong but not how to fix it
- Interaction shortcuts that save a click but increase accidental answers
- Custom interaction patterns built as one-offs instead of added to the design system

**Big 4 check:** "How do I move forward?" "How do I go back?" Is it clear when an action is final? When something goes wrong, does the user know what happened and how to fix it?

## Guardrail: For anyone, anywhere

Users can access everything we make, in their language, with their data kept safe.

**Example:** Learner: Read-aloud and extra time are built into the test, not requested separately.

**Rules out:**

- Meaning shown by color alone
- "We'll do accessibility later"
- Student data kept or used without a clear reason, or without telling users why

**Big 4 check:** Can every user answer all four questions, including with a keyboard, a screen reader, or in another language?
